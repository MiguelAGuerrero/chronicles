# Index every stop's hours, tile by tile

A look (Nov 22, 2026) at how chronicles-data could build, incrementally, a full index of the stops a walker can enter in every US town (shops worth browsing, museums, galleries, markets, attractions), each carrying its opening hours where a source states them, and later the world. The owner set the bounds: open data plus businesses' own websites, crawled politely; no commercial APIs; costs in tiers. Nothing here is adopted. **The short answer: OpenStreetMap is the only open source of hours for independent shops and museums, AllThePlaces (CC0) is the only open source of chain hours, and neither Overture nor Foursquare's open tier carries hours at all; a business's own website is the one way to widen coverage, and it should be read structured-data-first, from archived copies before live ones, with a language model only as a guarded last step.** The architecture the pipeline already has (adapters, a raw store, conflation with opaque IDs, a gate, packs per z8 block) takes this with one addition, a lease-based task table for the crawl, and no new orchestrator. The US at the owner's scope ("shops worth browsing; errands and services stay out", decision 0221) fits the free tier: GitHub Actions, the Pages site and, if the owner wants a language-model fallback, about $9 for a first full pass. What the app does with hours is not settled and must not be guessed here: SHF29 waits on a measured `withHours` coverage that **does not exist yet**, because no OpenStreetMap run has happened since the counting code landed; Monday's weekly build (Nov 23) or a hand run will produce it.

## Only two open sources state hours, and the measured share is still missing

The candidate datasets split cleanly into those that state hours, those that only confirm a place exists, and those to skip. Overture Places (90,295,748 places in the Nov 2026 release) has **no opening-hours property in its schema at all**; its `operating_status` (open, temporarily_closed, permanently_closed) "should not be confused with opening hours" ([Overture place schema](https://github.com/OvertureMaps/schema/blob/main/packages/overture-schema-theme-places/src/overture/schema/places/place.py); [Nov 2026 release notes](https://github.com/OvertureMaps/docs/blob/main/blog/2026-11-18-release-notes.mdx)). Foursquare OS Places keeps `hours` in its Pro and Premium schemas, not the open one, and since October 2025 sits behind a sign-in portal and a gated Hugging Face dataset ([Places Pro and Premium](https://docs.foursquare.com/data-products/docs/places-pro-and-premium), snippet only; [HF dataset](https://huggingface.co/datasets/foursquare/fsq-os-places)). AllThePlaces scrapes brands' own store locators weekly and publishes CC0 GeoJSON with an OSM-style `opening_hours` wherever a spider decodes it ([ATP README](https://github.com/alltheplaces/alltheplaces/blob/master/README.md); [DATA_FORMAT.md](https://github.com/alltheplaces/alltheplaces/blob/master/DATA_FORMAT.md)); it is chain-biased by construction, and Museum and TouristAttraction are not among its structured-data spider's default types ([structured_data_spider.py](https://github.com/alltheplaces/alltheplaces/blob/master/locations/structured_data_spider.py)). That leaves OSM's `opening_hours` as the only open statement of hours for the independent bookshops, antique dealers and small museums that are the pack's core.

Taginfo, which would give a US split, was blocked from this session. Every coverage-driven choice below is written so the Nov 23 report can settle it; none should be made on an estimate.

## The pipeline already has the right shape; the crawl adds a task table

The recommended flow is the one decision 0108 set, with the crawl as one more adapter: **sources → raw store → conflation with stable opaque IDs → gate → packs → publish only changed blocks.** Each source keeps its own cadence (OSM weekly, ATP weekly, Overture monthly through its changelog, Wikidata a rotating share of cells, registers on their release dates, websites on a freshness-driven revisit). Overture's changelog marks every feature added, removed or data_changed with `columns_changed` ([GERS changelog](https://github.com/OvertureMaps/docs/blob/main/docs/gers/changelog.mdx)), and because a release stays online only about 60 days ([release calendar](https://github.com/OvertureMaps/docs/blob/main/docs/release-calendar.mdx)), the adapter must copy the US slice it needs into the raw store each month; DuckDB reads it by bounding box straight from the sponsored S3 bucket at no egress cost ([AWS Registry: Overture](https://registry.opendata.aws/overture/)).

**The z8 tile is the unit of work, storage and publishing; towns are a lookup and priority layer, not the partition.** The manifest already hashes each z8 block so the app rebuilds only areas whose places changed (`chronicles-data/README.md`, ST7, ST10). A z8 tile at 40°N is about 120 km on a side, so the lower 48 need about 560 land tiles and the whole US roughly 800–1,000 non-empty ones, the same order as the 630 Wikidata cells already fetched; the world has 65,536 z8 tiles of which roughly 15–25k hold land (arithmetic in the orchestration notes, from 360/256 = 1.406° per tile). Town polygons overlap (CDPs inside counties), leave gaps (rural stops in no place) and change yearly, so a town partition would need a second "outside any town" partition and would not line up with the published blocks. Towns earn their place as attributes: the Census Gazetteer's internal points join each incorporated place and CDP to its tile, giving a town list per tile for reporting ("which towns does this block cover") and a priority signal (re-crawl dense towns sooner); abroad, GeoNames cities500 (about 185k places) plays the same part, its CC-BY licence unverified this session ([GeoNames readme](https://android.googlesource.com/platform/external/geonames/+/7a853d30915d91f323de5649b130afbd00e609de/export/dump/readme.txt)).

**Conflation runs per z8 tile, keys first, then scores.** OSM joins Overture through Overture's bridge files without a matcher ("join your OSM IDs to record_id under provider=osm") ([Bridge Files](https://docs.overturemaps.org/gers/bridge-files/), snippet); Wikidata joins OSM through the `wikidata=` tag; ATP joins Overture through Overture's own ATP source records and OSM through `brand:wikidata` and `ref`. Only what remains needs scored matching: block on a small cell or a name token within 250 m, then compare normalised names, distance bands and kind compatibility, with a shared website domain or phone as strong evidence. A US tile averages about 11k stops per source, and even dense tiles sit far inside what a per-tile matcher handles. Note the conflict with 0108's "Node and TypeScript only": Splink, the obvious probabilistic matcher on DuckDB ([Splink](https://pypi.org/project/splink)), is Python, and 0108 says Python tools wait "until rule-based matching falls short". Rule-based matching in TypeScript comes first. **IDs stay ours**: GERS IDs change when a place is renamed or moves more than a few hundred metres ([GERS stability](https://github.com/OvertureMaps/docs/blob/main/docs/gers/stability.mdx)), so the registry maps each opaque `s_…` or place ID to the set of `{source}:{id}` members it has ever had; a rebuilt cluster inherits the ID holding most of its members, a split keeps the ID on the larger part, a merge keeps the older, and an ID is never minted from a hash of attributes. Stores and places keep separate registries and are never merged by the matcher (ST6); galleries and museums join the places pack as kinds of their own (SHF30).

**Orchestration stays on GitHub Actions with a plain lease table, not Dagster.** The prior research rejected a workflow server "for a handful of steps" (`docs/research/incremental-places-builds.md`), and the crawl does not change that answer: what it adds is millions of long-running, per-host rate-limited, resumable items, which a queue models and a DAG of partitions does not. One table (Parquet or SQLite in the state asset) holds `task_id, kind (source-tile | site), key (z8 or host), not_before, lease_until, lease_owner, attempts, last_status, etag, body_hash, hours_hash`; a lane claims rows whose lease has lapsed for 30 minutes and writes results back every few minutes, so a job killed at Actions' 6-hour cap ([Actions limits](https://docs.github.com/en/actions/reference/actions-limits)) loses one lease window. Per-host politeness lives in the scheduler (a `next_allowed_at` per registrable domain), so a chain with thousands of pages becomes one serial lane, and better still is left to ATP. The crawl becomes a source adapter whose raw store is that result table, with the extractor version in its fetch key and the existing `carry` keeping unchanged sites (`chronicles-data/src/adapter.ts`).

## Websites are read structured-first, archived-first, and model-last

The website step is seeded only from stops already located: the `website` tag in OSM and Overture's `websites` list. A website never puts a place on the map; a location found on a chain's sitemap that no locating source holds goes, at most, to the run's review list (0108). Chains are not crawled at all: ATP asks others not to re-run its spiders ("if we all started doing this then a site is far more likely to engage blocking measures") and publishes them CC0 ([ATP weekly run](https://github.com/alltheplaces/alltheplaces/blob/master/docs/WEEKLY_RUN.md)). What remains is the long tail of independents with one site and one page, for which a single "one-site" fetcher fits better than ATP's spider-per-brand model.

The order of reads matters more than any tool. **First, archived copies, which touch no business's server.** Web Data Commons' October 2024 extraction found a LocalBusiness subset of **1,456,650 hosts and 27.2M URLs** ([WDC 2024 subsets](https://www.webdatacommons.org/structureddata/2024-12/stats/schema_org_subsets.html)); in 2022, 19.2M OpeningHoursSpecification entities sat beside 55.9M LocalBusiness entities, about one in three ([WDC 2022 subsets](https://webdatacommons.org/structureddata/2022-12/stats/schema_org_subsets.html)). No 2025 WDC release was found. Common Crawl's URL index can be queried with Athena at $5 per TiB scanned, **at most about $1.50 for a whole monthly index**, and a domain-filtered query costs fractions of a cent; the matching WARC record is then range-fetched from the archive ([Common Crawl columnar index](https://commoncrawl.org/blog/index-to-warc-files-and-urls-in-columnar-format); [skeptric](https://www.skeptric.com/common-crawl-index-athena)). Both only see sites CCBot sampled and that did not block it, so expect good cover of chains and template sites and poor cover of small independents; one Athena join of the stops' domains measures it. **Second, a polite live fetch** of what the archive lacks: robots.txt per RFC 9309, treating a 5xx on robots.txt as full disallow ([RFC 9309](https://datatracker.ietf.org/doc/html/rfc9309)); an identifying user agent with a project URL and an opt-out page on the Pages site; one connection per host and at least ATP's 1-second delay, honouring a larger `Crawl-delay` though it is not standard ([ATP settings.py](https://github.com/alltheplaces/alltheplaces/blob/master/locations/settings.py)); backing off on 429/503; no logins, no clicked terms, no CAPTCHA solving, no headless evasion; and at most about four requests a site per pass (robots.txt, the homepage, up to two pages whose link reads hours, visit or contact), with `If-None-Match`/`If-Modified-Since` so an unchanged page costs a 304.

**Extraction is a ladder, and each rung records how it got its answer** (`jsonld | microdata | text-regex | llm`). Structured data comes first: JSON-LD is used by 70% of sites that annotate ([Univ. Mannheim](https://www.bwl.uni-mannheim.de/en/details/wdc-json-ld-microdata-rdfa-data-corpus-2024-published/), snippet), and schema.org's `openingHoursSpecification` maps mechanically to OSM syntax, which ATP's MIT-licensed `OpeningHours` class already does in about 40 languages ([ATP hours.py](https://github.com/alltheplaces/alltheplaces/blob/master/locations/hours.py)); for a Node pipeline that code is a model to port, not a dependency. Only the hours properties are read; everything else in the markup, ratings and price levels included, is dropped, as ST4 already does for OSM tags. Next, a regex pass for day and time patterns on trimmed page text. A Google Maps embed is skipped outright: its hours are Google's content under Google's terms ([Maps Platform terms](https://cloud.google.com/maps-platform/terms/maps-service-terms/index-20240422)). **A language model is the last rung and runs only under guards**: on a page with day and time patterns but no structured data, on text trimmed to a window around them, with a forced output schema, a required `null` when unsure, and a quoted `evidence` span that must occur verbatim in the source text; the answer must then parse as OSM `opening_hours` without warnings and total under 168 hours a week, or it is discarded. Input format drives hallucination: one 2025 benchmark saw F1 of 0.9567 on flat JSON input against a hallucination rate of 0.5976 on raw HTML in one configuration ([NEXT-EVAL, arXiv 2505.17125](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.17125)), and no benchmark specific to hours exists, so a pilot of about 500 hand-labelled pages has to measure accuracy before any model-derived hours ship. Per Anthropic's pricing page (read Nov 22), **Haiku 5.5 on the Batch API costs $0.05 per million input tokens and $0.25 per million output tokens** for prompts up to 100k tokens, a tenth of Haiku 4.5's batch $0.50/$2.50 ([Anthropic pricing](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing)); at about 2,000 input and 100 output tokens a trimmed page, that is roughly **$0.12–0.15 per 1,000 pages**, the upper figure allowing the page's note that newer models' tokenizer uses about 30% more tokens, an uplift the page does not state for Haiku 5.5 by name.

Hours are carried "in the source's own form" (SHF28), and the derivation is kept beside them so a rule change re-derives rather than patches, the same truth-versus-cache line the app draws. Validation needs a parser: opening_hours.js 3.16.0 (LGPL-3.0, Nov 16, 2026) is the reference evaluator and carries public and school holiday calendars ([npm opening_hours](https://registry.npmjs.org/opening_hours)); the Rust crate `opening-hours` 2.1.6 and its Python bindings are MIT/Apache-2.0 with an embedded holiday database ([crates.io](https://crates.io/crates/opening-hours); [PyPI](https://pypi.org/project/opening-hours-py/)). Under 0108's Node-only rule, opening_hours.js fits the build; its LGPL matters only if the app bundles it, which is the owner's library choice (spec open question 6).

## Hours are a dated statement, and a failed read is never "closed"

Freshness is the part where the product rules bite hardest. Business churn alone outpaces any survey: **34.7% of US establishments born in 2013 still operated in 2023** ([BLS TED](https://www.bls.gov/opub/ted/2024/34-7-percent-of-business-establishments-born-in-2013-were-still-operating-in-2023.htm)), and no study was found of how often a shop's posted hours change. OSM's only freshness signal is `check_date:opening_hours`, which StreetComplete reads to re-ask about hours after about a year ([StreetComplete issue 3638](https://www.github.com/streetcomplete/StreetComplete/issues/3638)). The model that follows: every observation (URL or OSM element, fetch date, HTTP status, method, evidence span, OSM string) is append-only truth in the raw store, and the published hours are derived from it. Each carried hours value travels with **its source and a checked month** (`check_date:opening_hours` for OSM, or the element's last edit if the extract keeps timestamps, which is unverified; the fetch date for a website; the run date for ATP). The owner sets the age past which a value is dropped rather than shown; the evidence suggests about a year for OSM and about 8 weeks unconfirmed for a website.

The hard rule is that **no failure becomes "closed"**. A partial parser gives confidently wrong answers: Organic Maps' old parser read `Su,PH off` as closed on Sunday, and its replacement, a C++ port of opening-hours-rs, landed in 2026 ([OSM Community thread](https://community.openstreetmap.org/t/comaps-sorgt-fur-unvollstandige-offnungszeiten/146392), excerpt; which project ships it is unconfirmed). For SHF29's option of leaving out a place its source says is closed today, that is the bigger risk, so the build keeps three states (parsed, unparsed-kept-raw, absent), and only a parse that positively yields `off` or no matching rule may count as closed; an unparsed value says nothing as a time, and a stop with none says "Opening hours aren't known here." Closure of the business, as distinct from today's hours, is a different signal ranked by strength: explicit dated text ("permanently closed"), an Overture `permanently_closed` (chosen by majority vote across its sources since October 2026; [Oct 2026 notes](https://github.com/OvertureMaps/docs/blob/main/blog/2026-10-21-release-notes.mdx)), an ATP location absent two runs running, HTTP 410, then a dead or parked domain across two runs. Overture's own history shows the trap: it shipped `operating_status` as "open" everywhere in September 2025 and reset it to blank in May 2026 ([May 2026 notes](https://docs.overturemaps.org/blog/2026/05/20/release-notes/), excerpt). A null means unknown. Holidays are evaluated on the phone from a `PH` rule and the place's country and state, which the pack can carry; where no `PH` rule exists the honest line is silence or "holiday hours may differ", the same doubt Google marks on an unconfirmed holiday ([Google Maps blog](https://maps.googleblog.com/2015/11/tis-season-google-brings-holiday-hours_6.html)).

The revisit is driven by staleness, cheapest check first. Priority is days since the last check over a revisit target (4 weeks for a site whose hours changed in its last two visits, 8 by default, 16 after three unchanged visits); a 304 costs one request, a changed raw hash costs a parse, and only a changed hours hash touches the conflated record and the z8 block hash, so a page whose text changed but whose hours did not publishes nothing (ST10). None of this reaches the phone as a schedule: the pack holds a string, a source and a month, never an `openNow` boolean (ST1), and whatever the page does with it stays a statement about the place, never a countdown or a prompt to hurry (SHF29).

## Three cost tiers, and the owner's scope keeps the US in the free one

The crawl budget scales with how many stops are in scope, and the owner's scope rule is the lever. Decision 0221 keeps errands and services out, so the target is "stops worth entering", not every business; the planning figure below assumes about 300k US stops (a placeholder until the Nov 23 counts and one DuckDB query of Overture's US `websites` share replace it), against roughly 10M for every US POI. The assumptions are stated so they can be swapped: 40% of stops have a website, four requests a site, about 50 KB a response, 60% lacking structured hours and so reaching the model, about 2,000 input and 100 output tokens a page, and 15% of revisited pages changing between passes.

|---|---|---|---|
| Sites (× 0.40) | 120k | 4M | 40M |
| Requests a pass (× 4) | 480k | 16M | 160M |
| Polite wall-clock | 480k ÷ 10 req/s ≈ 13 h | 18.5 days at 10 req/s; 3.7 at 50 | ≈ 37 days at 50 req/s |

| Pages to the model (× 0.6) | 72k | 2.4M | 24M |

| Model cost, gated pass (× 0.15) | ≈ $1–2 | ≈ $45 | ≈ $450 |

**Tier 1, free ($0 a month, plus at most about $1.50 a Common Crawl query and a one-off $9 if the model rung is used).** OSM weekly, ATP weekly, Overture monthly by bounding box, Wikidata by rotating cells, the registers, and a stops-in-scope crawl on a 4-week rotation: 480k requests over 28 days is about 17k a night, one nightly Actions job of about 30 minutes at 10 req/s. Standard runners are free in public repositories with 20 concurrent jobs ([Actions billing](https://docs.github.com/en/billing/managing-billing-for-github-actions/about-billing-for-github-actions)); three versions of a 30–40 MB pack sit well inside GitHub Pages' 1 GB site limit ([Pages limits](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/getting-started-with-github-pages/github-pages-limits)). Skip the model rung and the tier is $0, at the cost of every independent site whose hours are only prose. Whether GitHub's acceptable-use terms allow a crawler on Actions was not verified and should be read first.

**Tier 2, small monthly (about $10–35).** Every US POI on an 8-week rotation, 16M requests over 56 days or about 3.3 req/s on average, which one small VPS sustains: Hetzner's CX33 is €8.49 and CAX21 €10.49 a month after its June 2026 price rise ([Hetzner price adjustment](https://docs.hetzner.com/general/infrastructure-and-availability/price-adjustment/)). The alternative is Cloudflare Workers ($5 base) with Queues at $0.40 per million operations past the first million, three operations a message: 16M × 3 = 48M operations, (48 − 1) × $0.40 = $18.80, plus about $1 of CPU, so about $25 a pass ([Queues pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/queues/pricing/); [Workers pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/workers/platform/pricing/)). The model's first pass is about $300, then about $45 every 8 weeks, about $22 a month. The 1.2 GB pack breaks the Pages limit, so packs and state move to R2 at $0.015 per GB-month with 10 GB free and no egress ([R2 pricing](https://developers.cloudflare.com/r2/pricing/)): three versions at 3.6 GB cost nothing. Totals: about €10 + $0–1 + $22 ≈ **$35 a month**, or about $10 without the model.

**Tier 3, paid best coverage (about $270–500 a month).** The world in per-region packs, 160M requests a pass on a 4–8 week rotation (33–66 req/s on average) across 2–4 VPS at about €40, the model at about $450 a gated pass, so $225–450 a month, and R2 storage for three 12 GB versions at (36 − 10) × $0.015 ≈ $0.39 a month, with reads inside the free 10M Class B operations until well past 10k users and about $7.20 a month at 100k. Structured data only brings this tier down to about $50 a month. The 2 GiB release-asset cap ([About releases](https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/releasing-projects-on-github/about-releases)) already forces state to be sharded by region, and Overture's free egress rests on a sponsorship with "no assurance that Amazon's sponsorship will be perpetual" ([AWS Open Data FAQ](https://aws.amazon.com/opendata/faqs/)).

## Licences mix cleanly into ODbL; the legal weak spot is EU chain locators

Licence mixing is not the blocker. The stores pack is already ODbL because it is built from OSM, and a pack conflating OSM with Overture's CDLA-Permissive-2.0, Apache-2.0 and CC0 components stays an ODbL derivative ([Overture Places guide](https://github.com/OvertureMaps/docs/blob/main/docs/guides/places/index.mdx)); ATP's CC0 and US federal public-domain data add no obligation; Foursquare's Apache-2.0 part, reached through Overture, needs its NOTICE line; RIDB is CC BY 4.0 and needs credit ([data.gov RIDB](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/recreation-information-database-ridb-58364)); abroad, France's Licence Ouverte 2.0 and Canada's OGL are attribution licences compatible with ODbL, and Australia's ATDW forbids redistribution and is out. Each goes into `sources.json` with its attribution as today.

Website-extracted hours carry low risk in the US and a specific one in the EU. Facts are not copyrightable in the US (Feist, [499 U.S. 340](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/499/340/)); logged-out scraping of public pages falls outside the CFAA after hiQ ([Proskauer](https://www.proskauer.com/blog/hiq-and-linkedin-reach-proposed-settlement-in-landmark-scraping-case)); browsewrap terms did not bind a logged-out scraper in Meta v. Bright Data ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/01/24/court-rules-in-favor-of-a-web-scraper-bright-data-which-meta-had-used-and-then-sued)); and trespass to chattels needs actual impairment of the server, which a polite crawl avoids. In the EU the risk is the sui generis database right over a substantial part of a database whose making took substantial investment (CV-Online Latvia v Melons, [Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2021/global/cv-online-latvia-complicating-database-right-enforcement)): one shop's hours box is not that, a chain's 2,000-store locator plausibly is. The DSM Directive's Art. 4 text-and-data-mining exception covers extraction unless the rightholder reserves it by machine-readable means, which Kneschke v LAION read as robots.txt or metadata (OLG Hamburg, Dec 10, 2025; [Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/germany/higher-regional-court-hamburg-confirms-ai-training-was-permitted-(kneschke-v,-d-,-laion)); one commentator reads it the other way and a BGH appeal is pending), but Art. 4 does not plainly cover re-utilisation, publishing what was extracted. The safe course abroad is to take chains from ATP's CC0 output rather than crawl their locators, honour robots.txt and TDMRep's `tdm-reservation` ([W3C TDMRep report](https://www.w3.org/community/reports/tdmrep/CG-FINAL-tdmrep-20240510/)) as opt-outs even where fact extraction might not need the exception, and skip a site whose terms say no scraping. For a sole trader whose hours describe a named person's availability, GDPR may apply; publishing only the hours of premises, never names, phones or emails, keeps that out (an inference; no source fetched). One question is new: page text is the site owner's copyright, so raw HTML must not go into the public `state-us` pre-release that 0108 keeps for redistributable sources; only fetch metadata, hashes, the derived string and the short evidence span should, and whether even the span belongs there is the owner's call.

The main risks, in order: a partial parse that hides an open shop as closed (guarded by the three states and the positive-`off` rule); stale hours from churn and unrecorded changes (guarded by the checked month and the drop age); model hallucination (guarded by verbatim spans, parse validation and the pilot); chain bias in what has hours (independents depend on OSM and the crawl); the crawl's reputation, since one impolite pass can get the project's agent blocked everywhere ATP warns of; EU database-right exposure on chain locators; Actions' acceptable-use terms for crawling (unverified); and dependencies outside the project's control, from Overture's sponsored egress to Foursquare's access gate to IMLS, which survived a 2025 attempt to close it only by a court order and an April 2026 settlement ([ALA](https://www.ala.org/news/2026/04/trump-administration-withdraws-appeal-securing-historic-victory-libraries-and-imls)).

## Conclusion

The question looked like a crawling problem and turns out to be a scope and honesty problem. The owner's own rule, stops worth entering and not every business, shrinks the US crawl from weeks to a nightly half hour and keeps it free; and the vision's rule that a time is a statement, never a schedule, turns freshness from a ranking signal into a dated sentence the pipeline can always back with its source. The one genuinely new thing the plan introduces is a source with no licence of its own, a business's website, and the cleanest way to admit it is the narrowest: an attribute of a place another source already located, read from its structured data, archived first, with the model rung earned by a measured pilot rather than assumed.

What changes the plan most is a number nobody has yet. If Monday's `withHours` shows OSM already stating hours for most US museums and a fair share of independent shops, Phase 1 alone may serve SHF29 and the crawl becomes a slow supplement; if it shows a thin sliver, the website pilot is where coverage comes from and the owner's choice of parser and of how hours are used matters sooner.
